How to Break the Deadlock in a China JV and Exit Cleanly

June 13, 2026 Sofos | & Partners PRC

Introduction

In better market conditions, many joint ventures can survive inefficiency. Shareholders may disagree, meetings may be delayed, and operational problems may be tolerated because the business still has momentum.

But today's market is different.

As economic pressure increases, we are seeing more "zombie company" situations in China: companies that no longer operate, no longer generate value, and no longer have functioning governance — yet still remain legally alive. But unless the company is properly dissolved, liquidated, and de-registered, it continues to exist as a legal entity. That means compliance obligations, possible abnormal-operation records, tax complications, creditor risks, and potential pressure relating to subscribed registered capital may continue.

For foreign investors, this can become a serious trap.

A Typical Deadlock Scenario

To give an example which we handled: company A was a limited liability company with registered capital of RMB 10 million. Our client is one of the two shareholders. The company had been established for several years, but it no longer carries out substantive business operation. Most challenging part is that: the other shareholder had become unreachable.

This left our client in a difficult position. The company was no longer doing business, but it could not be voluntarily deregistered. Shareholders' meetings could not be effectively convened. No valid resolutions could be passed. The company had become a classic zombie company: no business, no functioning governance, and no practical internal exit mechanism.

How We Solved It

After reviewing the company documents, capital structure, tax status, business records, and evidence of failed communication, we advised that the most practical route was not further negotiation, but judicial dissolution followed by liquidation.

We filed a company dissolution lawsuit before a district court in Shanghai on behalf of the minority shareholder. Although the other shareholder failed to appear despite proper summons, the court accepted that the company had serious management difficulties, that continued existence would harm the shareholder's interests, and that the deadlock could not be resolved through ordinary internal procedures.

The court issued a judgment dissolving the company. After the judgment became effective, the case moved into the liquidation stage. A court-supervised liquidation process was initiated, a liquidation group was designated, and the company was ultimately de-registered in June 2025. From filing to final deregistration, the full process took approximately ten months.

The Legal Basis: Judicial Dissolution

Under Article 231 of the PRC Company Law, where a company faces serious difficulties in operation and management, continued existence would cause major loss to shareholders' interests, and the problem cannot be solved by other means, shareholders holding 10% or more of the voting rights may request the People's Court to dissolve the company.

This remedy is especially important in deadlocked joint ventures. However, judicial dissolution is not granted simply because a company is losing money or because shareholders are unhappy. The court focuses on whether the company's governance mechanism has failed.

Typical indicators include:

  • Shareholders' meetings cannot be convened for a long period
  • Meetings may be called, but no valid resolution can be passed
  • Voting thresholds under the articles of association cannot be reached
  • Shareholders or directors are in prolonged conflict
  • One shareholder is missing, unreachable, detained, bankrupt, or refuses to cooperate
  • The company has no real business and no realistic path to voluntary deregistration

Practical Lessons for Foreign Investors

First, a deadlock clause in a joint venture contract is useful, but it may not be enough. If the other shareholder disappears or refuses to cooperate, contractual mechanisms may be impossible to implement without court involvement.

Second, evidence matters. Meeting notices, delivery records, emails, social media messages, lawyer's letters, failed meeting minutes, tax records, business-status records, and proof of non-operation can all be important.

Third, timing matters. If the company has been unable to hold meetings or pass resolutions for a long period, investors should preserve evidence instead of waiting passively.

Fourth, dissolution is not the final goal. The real goal is deregistration. A complete strategy should cover dissolution, liquidation, and cancellation.

Finally, even minority shareholders have remedies. A shareholder holding at least 10% of voting rights may have standing to seek judicial dissolution if the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Conclusion

A deadlocked China joint venture can be frustrating, especially when the other shareholder is missing, hostile, bankrupt, detained, or simply unwilling to cooperate. But a zombie company does not have to remain a permanent burden. Where the company's governance has failed, continued existence would harm shareholders, and no practical internal solution remains, judicial dissolution followed by liquidation and deregistration may provide a clear legal exit.

For investors, the key is to move from frustration to evidence, and from evidence to a structured legal process. In today's tougher market environment, zombie companies are becoming more common. The earlier they are diagnosed and handled properly, the easier it is to prevent them from becoming long-term legal and financial liabilities.`,
