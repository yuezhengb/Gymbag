China Company Revocation vs. Deregistration: When Is a Company Really Born and When Does It Legally Die?

July 2, 2026 Sofos | & Partners PRC

Q: When is a company legally established in China?
A company is legally established on the date its business license is issued. Under China's Company Law, the issuance date of the business license is the date of establishment of the company (see Art. 33, PRC Company Law).

Q: If a company's business license is revoked, does the company cease to exist?
No. Revocation of the business license normally means the company has lost its business operation qualification. It does not, by itself, extinguish the company's legal personality. The company generally remains in existence for liquidation purposes until liquidation is completed and deregistration is filed (see Art. 68, PRC Civil Code; Arts. 229, 232, 236 and 239, PRC Company Law).

Q: Why does this matter for foreign investors, parent companies, and China market exits?
Because "revoked but not deregistered" is not a clean exit. It can become a due diligence issue, a tax issue, a litigation issue, and in some cases a personal risk issue for the legal representative, directors, senior officers, or liquidation obligors.

The old assumption: no license, no company

For years, many business teams have worked with a simple assumption: if a Chinese company has a business license, it exists; if it no longer has a valid business license, it has disappeared.

The first half is generally right. The second half is where companies get caught.

China's business license has a dual function. It evidences the company's legal-person status and also its qualification to conduct business. That is why the issuance of the business license is the legal marker of corporate birth (see Art. 33, PRC Company Law).

But the reverse logic does not work. Losing the license does not necessarily mean losing legal personality. A company may have no right to continue ordinary business operations, but it may still exist as a legal person for liquidation, debt settlement, litigation, tax clearance, employee matters, and asset disposal.

That distinction is not academic. It is often discovered late, usually during financing, restructuring, M&A, or a new China market entry.

Where foreign companies usually discover the problem

A foreign parent company once had a Chinese subsidiary. The business stopped operating years ago. The local team changed. The finance manager left. The company seal, tax credentials, bank account, original contracts, and accounting books were no longer actively managed.

No one formally liquidated the company. No one completed tax deregistration. No one filed company deregistration with the registration authority.

Several years later, the parent company prepares for a financing, restructuring, M&A transaction, or a new China project. During due diligence, the old entity appears in the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System as "revoked, not deregistered".

At that point, the problem is no longer only a registration issue. If the company has unpaid taxes, unresolved litigation, enforcement proceedings, missing accounting books, or a failed liquidation, the risk may travel to individuals who were registered or treated as responsible persons — for example, the legal representative, directors, senior management personnel, principal responsible persons, directly responsible personnel, or liquidation obligors.

In more serious cases, this may affect their ability to serve in future China entities, trigger credit or enforcement consequences, or even lead to an exit restriction from China where the statutory conditions are met. For example, where a taxpayer or its legal representative has unpaid taxes and fails to settle the tax, late payment surcharge, or provide security before leaving China, the tax authority may notify the exit-entry authority to prevent departure (see Art. 44, PRC Tax Collection and Administration Law).

For Chinese citizens and foreign nationals, China's Exit and Entry Administration Law also contains exit-restriction grounds where there is an unresolved civil case and a people's court decides that the person may not leave China (see Arts. 12 and 28, PRC Exit and Entry Administration Law).

Revocation alone does not automatically mean that a parent-company employee will be stopped at the border. But a company left in the status of "revoked but not deregistered" can turn an old corporate housekeeping issue into a personal mobility, governance, and transaction-clearance issue.

The legal pivot: revocation is not deregistration

The Chinese term diaoxiao is often translated as "revocation." In this context, it usually refers to the administrative revocation of a business license.

The term zhuxiao is different. It means deregistration. In company exit practice, it refers to the formal removal of the company from the company registration system after the required legal steps have been completed.

The Company Law treats revocation of a business license as a statutory cause of dissolution. A company is dissolved if its business license is revoked, it is ordered to close, or it is revoked by law (see Art. 229, PRC Company Law).

But dissolution is not termination.

Once a dissolution event occurs, the company must go through liquidation. Under the current Company Law, directors are the liquidation obligors, and they must form a liquidation group within 15 days after the dissolution event occurs, unless the articles of association or a shareholders' resolution provides otherwise (see Art. 232, PRC Company Law).

During liquidation, the company continues to exist, but it may not conduct business activities unrelated to liquidation. Its assets must be used to pay liquidation expenses, employee wages, social insurance contributions, statutory compensation, unpaid taxes, and company debts before any remaining assets are distributed to shareholders (see Art. 236, PRC Company Law).

Only after liquidation is completed should the liquidation group prepare a liquidation report and file for company deregistration with the registration authority (see Art. 239, PRC Company Law).

This is why the more precise rule is:

Issuance of the business license creates the company. Revocation of the business license does not kill the company. Completion of liquidation and deregistration does.

Why the distinction exists

There is a policy reason behind this structure.

If a company could disappear merely because its business license was revoked, creditors, employees, tax authorities, contractual counterparties, and courts would be left without a responsible legal subject. The company could lose the right to operate and, at the same time, escape the duty to clean up its debts.

Chinese law avoids that result by separating business qualification from legal personality.

Revocation removes the company from normal business life. Deregistration removes the company from legal life.

Between those two points, the company is in a narrow legal corridor: it exists, but only for the purpose of liquidation and legally necessary winding-up matters.

The personal risk: qualification, exit restrictions, and criminal tax exposure

A revoked-but-not-deregistered entity can also create individual-level risk.

First, the legal representative and senior personnel may face qualification restrictions in certain circumstances. For example, a person who served as legal representative of a company or enterprise whose business license was revoked or which was ordered to close due to legal violations, and who bears personal responsibility, may not serve as a director, supervisor, or senior officer for three years from the date of revocation or closure (see Art. 178, PRC Company Law).

Second, where unpaid taxes are involved, the risk may become more personal. A company's legal representative may face exit restriction if the taxpayer has outstanding taxes and fails to settle them or provide security before departure (see Art. 44, PRC Tax Collection and Administration Law).

Third, where there is pending civil litigation or enforcement, a court-ordered exit restriction may become relevant. This is not caused by deregistration status alone. It is usually connected to unresolved proceedings, non-performance of effective legal documents, or concern that departure may frustrate litigation or enforcement (see Arts. 12 and 28, PRC Exit and Entry Administration Law).

Fourth, tax issues may move beyond administrative penalties in serious cases. For example, tax evasion may arise where a taxpayer uses deception or concealment to make false tax filings or fails to file, and the statutory thresholds are met (see Art. 201, PRC Criminal Law). Separately, the crime of evading recovery of tax arrears may arise where a taxpayer with tax arrears transfers or conceals assets so that the tax authority cannot recover the tax, again subject to statutory thresholds (see Art. 203, PRC Criminal Law). Where a unit commits certain tax-related crimes, the unit may be fined and the directly responsible persons may be punished under the relevant provisions (see Art. 211, PRC Criminal Law).

That does not mean every dormant or revoked company creates criminal exposure. It means that tax clearance should not be treated as a back-office formality. If the books are missing, invoices are irregular, assets were transferred, or the entity has been left outside tax supervision for years, the risk analysis should be done before anyone assumes the company can simply be abandoned.

The new pressure point: compulsory deregistration does not erase responsibility

The revised Company Law also introduces a compulsory deregistration mechanism. If a company has been revoked, ordered to close, or revoked by law, and fails to apply for deregistration for three years, the registration authority may make a public announcement through the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System. If no objection is raised after the announcement period, the authority may deregister the company. However, the responsibilities of the original shareholders and liquidation obligors are not affected (see Art. 241, PRC Company Law).

In other words: compulsory deregistration is not a liability release.

It may clean the public registry, but it does not necessarily clean the underlying legal history. This is the practical meaning of the phrase often used in China: "deregistering the account does not deregister responsibility".

What companies should do in practice

For foreign investors and parent companies, the first step is not to ask whether the old China entity is "still operating." The better question is: what is its registration, tax, litigation, enforcement, and liquidation status?

A practical review should cover at least five points.

  • First, check the public registration status: active, revoked but not deregistered, deregistered, ordered to close, or revoked by law.
  • Second, identify the registered legal representative, directors, supervisors, senior officers, shareholders, and any persons who may be viewed as responsible for liquidation.
  • Third, review tax status, including tax arrears, late payment surcharges, abnormal tax status, invoice history, missing filings, and whether tax deregistration can still be completed.
  • Fourth, review litigation, arbitration, enforcement, and creditor claims.
  • Fifth, map the exit route: ordinary liquidation and deregistration, court-supervised liquidation, bankruptcy liquidation, or — where legally available — simplified deregistration. A company that has been revoked or listed as abnormal may not be eligible for simplified deregistration in practice, so the route should be tested locally before documents are prepared.

Final takeaway

The mistake is not usually intentional. Most companies do not decide to ignore Chinese company law. They simply stop operating and assume that non-use equals non-existence.

It does not.

In China, corporate birth is relatively clear: the business license issuance date is the establishment date. Corporate death is more demanding: liquidation must be completed and deregistration must be filed.

For cross-border groups, that difference matters. A company that was never properly closed can reappear years later in financing, restructuring, M&A, tax clearance, immigration planning, or a new China project.

The goal is not only to close an old entity. The goal is to close the legal loop. Sooner is cheaper than later.
