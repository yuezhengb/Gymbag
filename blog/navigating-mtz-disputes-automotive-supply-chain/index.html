Navigating MTZ Disputes in the Global Automotive Supply Chain: A Comparative Legal Analysis of PRC and German Law

May 30, 2026 Sofos | & Partners PRC

Executive Summary

As the global automotive industry grapples with squeezed margins, geopolitical shifts, and the costly transition to electric vehicles (EVs), the traditional Material Price Surcharge (MTZ - Material Teuerungs Zuschlag) has transformed from a routine commercial adjustment tool into a critical legal battleground. For multinational OEMs and tier-suppliers, resolving these disputes requires a deep understanding of the governing laws. This insight provides a structural comparison of how MTZ disputes are treated under the People's Republic of China (PRC) Civil Code versus the German Civil Code (BGB), followed by strategic recommendations for supply chain stakeholders.

I. Structural Comparison: PRC Civil Code vs. German BGB

When fixed-price contracts lack explicit MTZ mechanisms, suppliers seeking relief must rely on statutory doctrines. Both jurisdictions prioritize keeping the contract alive through adaptation rather than termination, but their legal mechanics and thresholds for judicial intervention differ significantly.

II. Strategic Recommendations for the Supply Chain

To mitigate litigation risks and protect margins in either jurisdiction, automotive stakeholders must adopt proactive legal and commercial strategies:

Incorporate Mathematical Precision: Avoid vague "renegotiation upon inflation" clauses. Draft robust, bi-directional MTZ formulas directly tied to independent, recognized commodity indices (e.g., LME). Define precise trigger thresholds (e.g., a ±5% fluctuation).

Navigate the Open-Book Dilemma Carefully: Under PRC law, proving "manifest unfairness" often pushes suppliers toward disclosing their cost structures. Suppliers should implement ring-fenced auditing mechanisms to prove the specific impact of raw material inflation without exposing unrelated margins to OEM procurement teams.

Document the Engagement: Maintain a rigorous evidentiary trail. Both PRC and German courts look favorably upon parties that document their good-faith attempts to find alternative commercial solutions (such as VAVE engineering changes or relaxed tolerances) before escalating to legal claims.

Conclusion

The days of settling supply chain disputes by brute commercial force are drawing to a close. As MTZ conflicts intensify, a sophisticated grasp of cross-border legal frameworks is no longer a luxury—it is a baseline requirement. Whether your operations are governed by the German BGB or the PRC Civil Code, grounding your commercial strategy in airtight contractual mechanics and a meticulous paper trail of good-faith engagement is your strongest defense against margin erosion.
